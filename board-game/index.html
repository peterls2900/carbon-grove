Carbon Grove the board game

2–4 players each run a Gold Standard agroforestry project on a smallholder coffee farm. Every year brings world events and farm events. Only the difference between your project and the baseline earns credits.

Rules and what the game teaches

A round is one year

  • A world card is drawn: markets, regulation, climate. It affects every player.
  • Each player in turn chooses this year's spending, then draws a farm card (fire, drought, a farmer leaving, leakage…).
  • The trees grow. In years 5, 10, 15 and 20 a Performance Certification follows.
  • Players can sign buyer contracts on their turn by retiring GS VERs. First come, first served. Turn order rotates every year.

The baseline

Next to each project farm sits its baseline: the same farm where nothing is done. Credits are based on net removals = project stock − baseline stock − leakage. If the baseline would have regrown anyway (for example, after a coffee price crash leads farmers to abandon plots), your additionality shrinks. In the game the baseline moves every year, like a control plot. Many methodologies fix it at the start instead.

Gold Standard rules used

  • PERs (ex-ante) for up to 5 years of modelled net removals, at the start and after each Performance Certification without a shortfall.
  • 20% of all PERs and GS VERs go to the Compliance Buffer.
  • Verification converts PERs into GS VERs first; any surplus becomes new GS VERs.
  • Units issued must not exceed net removals. Otherwise it's a shortfall: no new issuance until fixed. Not fixed within 5 years means a non-conformity.
  • Only GS VERs can be retired. PERs can be transferred but not retired.

Victory points

  • 1 VP per $2,000 cash (negative cash costs points)
  • 1 VP per 50 t retired for buyers
  • 1 VP per 100 t net removals still standing at the end
  • 1 VP per 2 years of farmer programme
  • −3 VP per shortfall, −10 VP for a non-conformity, −1 VP per 50 PERs never converted

Simplified teaching model. Prices, growth, events and the world cards are invented, and the buffer-rate change card is fictional.
